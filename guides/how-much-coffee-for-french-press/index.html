How Much Coffee for French Press: Grams and Scoops by Press Size

How much coffee for French press, by press size: grams, tablespoons and scoops for 12, 17, 34 and 51 oz presses, plus mild, standard and strong ratios.

Use about 1 gram of coffee for every 15 grams of water (1:15). In everyday terms that's roughly 2 grams of coarse ground coffee per fluid ounce of water, or about 16 g (3 level tablespoons) per 8 oz cup. A full 34 oz press takes about 57 g of coffee, and a small 12 oz press takes about 20 g. Go toward 1:12 for stronger coffee and 1:17 for lighter coffee.

The tricky part isn't the ratio. It's that French press sizes are labeled in small 4 oz "cups", that you can't fill a press to its full rated volume, and that a tablespoon of coffee can weigh anywhere from 4 to 7 grams. The chart below already accounts for all three.

The water column is how much you can actually brew, about 85% of the rated size, leaving room for the grounds and the plunger. Tablespoons assume about 5 g per level tablespoon of medium-roast coffee ground coarse. Water weight and volume match: 1 gram of water is 1 milliliter.

Making less than a full press is fine. Work from the water you want: multiply the grams of water by 0.067 for the standard ratio (that's the same as dividing by 15). For two 8 oz mugs, 475 g of water needs about 32 g of coffee.

Why the "cups" on your press don't mean mugs

Press makers count a cup as about 4 oz, the size of a small European coffee cup. So an "8 cup" press holds 34 oz in total and pours about three normal 10 oz mugs, not eight. If you follow a recipe that says "2 tablespoons per cup" and use the number printed on the box, you'll end up with weak coffee for fewer people than you planned.

Plan in mugs instead. A 12 oz press makes one mug. A 17 oz press makes one big mug or two small ones. A 34 oz press makes two to three mugs, and a 51 oz press makes four to five.

Measuring without a scale: tablespoons and scoops

A level tablespoon of coarse ground coffee usually weighs 5 to 6 g, but it depends on the roast and grind. Dark roast beans are lighter and puffier than light roasts, so a spoonful of dark roast holds less coffee. Coarse grounds also pack less tightly than fine ones. That's why the same "2 tablespoons" can taste strong one week and thin the next.

  • Per 8 oz cup of water: 3 level tablespoons (about 16 g), or 1.5 standard coffee scoops.
  • A standard coffee scoop is 2 tablespoons, so about 10 g. Check your scoop, though: many scoops sold with grinders and coffee tins are smaller.
  • Level the spoon with a knife or your finger. A heaped tablespoon can be 8 g or more.
  • Measure ground coffee, not whole beans. If you only have a spoon for beans, a level tablespoon of whole beans is about 5 g, close enough to start.
  • No measuring cup for water? A standard US measuring cup is 8 oz, or about 237 g.

The easy fix for all of this: weigh one level scoop of your usual coffee once on any kitchen scale, then write the number on a piece of tape on the canister. After that your scoop is as good as a scale until you change beans.

Standard, strong or mild: picking your ratio

Strength comes from the ratio. Taste comes from the grind and the steep time. Keep those two jobs separate and dialing in gets much easier.

  • 1:17 (mild): lighter body, good for light roasts you want to taste clearly, or for drinking several cups black.
  • 1:15 (standard): the usual starting point and what most coffee shops use for a press.
  • 1:12 (strong): heavier and richer. Good with milk or for dark roasts.
  • 1:10 or stronger: more of a concentrate. Fine if you top it up with hot water or milk, but a thick bed of grounds makes the plunger harder to push.

If stronger coffee is what you want, add coffee rather than grinding finer. A finer grind does make coffee taste stronger, but it also makes it bitter and muddy, and it clogs the mesh.

Be wary of older charts that suggest 7 to 8 g of coffee per 7 oz of water. That works out to roughly 1:26, about half the strength of the usual French press ratio, and it's a common reason home press coffee tastes weak.

How to brew once you've measured

  • Heat water to just off the boil, about 200°F (93°C). Thirty seconds after the kettle clicks off is close enough.
  • Grind coarse, like coarse sea salt. Add the coffee to the press.
  • Pour in all the water, or pour to the fill line on the beaker. Start a 4-minute timer.
  • Stir gently at about 1 minute to break up the crust, then put the lid on with the plunger raised.
  • At 4 minutes, press slowly over 15 to 30 seconds and pour all the coffee out. Coffee left on the grounds keeps getting stronger and more bitter.

The full method, including a slower and cleaner version, is in our guide to making French press coffee.

Common mistakes

  • Counting the cups printed on the press as mugs. A "cup" there is about 4 oz.
  • Filling the press to the brim. Leave about an inch at the top, or the plunger pushes coffee over the rim.
  • Using heaped spoons one day and level ones the next. Pick level and stick with it.
  • Switching from medium to dark roast without adjusting. Dark roast weighs less per spoon, so add a little more.
  • Making the coffee stronger by grinding finer. Add coffee instead.
  • Leaving the coffee in the press. Pour it into a mug or carafe right after pressing.

Tip: Make your first batch at 1:15 and change only one thing at a time. If you want it stronger, add 2 to 3 g of coffee per 300 g of water next time. Once you find your number, write it down with the grind setting so you can repeat it.

How many scoops of coffee for a 34 oz French press?

About 5 to 6 standard 2-tablespoon scoops (roughly 57 g) for about 29 oz of water, which is what fits in a 34 oz press with room for the grounds. Use 5 for a milder cup and 7 for a strong one.

How much coffee for one cup in a French press?

For an 8 oz cup, use about 16 g, or 3 level tablespoons. For a 10 oz mug, use about 20 g, or 4 level tablespoons.

What's the best coffee to water ratio for French press?

Start at 1:15 by weight, then adjust to taste anywhere between 1:12 (strong) and 1:17 (mild). Most people settle between 1:13 and 1:16.

Can I make a half batch in a big French press?

Yes. Use the same ratio for the smaller amount of water. A small batch in a big press cools faster, so rinse the press with hot water first, and keep the steep at about 4 minutes.

Does the amount of coffee change for cold brew in a French press?

Yes. Cold brew uses much more coffee, around 1:8 with a coarse grind, steeped 12 to 18 hours in the fridge. Press and pour it off, then dilute to taste.
