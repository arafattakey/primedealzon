Why Is My Coffee Bitter? 9 Causes and the Fix for Each

Why is my coffee bitter? Usually over-extraction. Find the cause in two minutes, with the first fix to try for drip, French press, pour-over and espresso.

Bitter coffee is almost always over-extracted coffee. The water pulled too much out of the grounds, usually because the grind was too fine, the coffee sat in the water too long, or the water was too hot. Start by grinding a step or two coarser. That one change fixes more bitter cups than anything else.

If that doesn't do it, the next suspects are stale or very dark beans, a dirty brewer, and coffee left sitting on a hot plate. Below is a quick way to tell bitter from sour (people mix them up all the time), the nine causes in the order worth checking, and the first fix to try for each brew method.

Bitter or sour? Check this first

Bitter and sour point in opposite directions, so getting this wrong makes things worse. Bitter is a dry, lingering harshness at the back of the tongue, like strong black tea or dark chocolate with no sugar. Sour is a sharp, puckering taste at the sides of the tongue, closer to an unripe lemon. A bitter cup usually also feels heavy and hollow. A sour one feels thin and salty.

A blade grinder chops beans into a mix of dust and chunks. The dust over-extracts and turns bitter while the chunks under-extract and taste sour, so you get both in the same cup and no grind setting fixes it.

The 9 causes, in the order to check them

1. The grind is too fine

Finer grounds have more surface area, so water extracts them faster. In drip and pour-over, fine grounds also slow the water down, which adds contact time on top. This is the most common cause by far. Move one or two settings coarser and brew again before you change anything else.

If you buy pre-ground coffee, check the bag. Coffee ground for espresso is far too fine for a drip machine or French press. Coffee labeled for drip is fine for most auto-drip machines but too fine for a French press.

2. The coffee brewed too long

Every method has a rough time window. Go well past it and bitter compounds take over. Typical targets:

  • French press: 4 minutes, then pour it all off the grounds right away.
  • Pour-over (V60 style, one cup): about 2:30 to 3:30 total.
  • Auto-drip machine: about 4 to 6 minutes for a full pot.
  • AeroPress: 1 to 2 minutes.
  • Espresso: about 25 to 30 seconds.
  • Moka pot: take it off the heat as soon as it starts to sputter.

The French press is the usual offender. If coffee sits on the grounds after you plunge, it keeps brewing. Pour everything into mugs or a carafe as soon as you plunge. Our French press brewing guide, linked at the end of this page, has the full recipe.

3. The water is too hot

The usual target is 195 to 205°F (90 to 96°C), just off the boil. Pouring water at a full rolling boil onto dark roasts in particular pulls out harsh flavors. With a stovetop kettle, wait about 30 seconds after it boils. With a variable-temperature kettle, try 200°F for medium roasts and 195°F for dark roasts.

Oddly, a drip machine with mineral scale inside can also run hot in places and cold in others, and it often brews slowly too. If your machine has started taking longer to brew a pot, descale it.

4. Too much coffee for the water

A very strong ratio tastes heavy and harsh rather than rich. A good starting point is 1:16 by weight, which is 60 to 65 g of coffee per liter of water, or about 2 level tablespoons per 6 oz cup. If you like it strong, try 1:15 before you go stronger than that. A scale takes the guessing out of this, because a scoop of dark roast and a scoop of light roast weigh different amounts.

5. The roast is very dark

Dark roasting turns more of the bean's sugars into bitter, roasty compounds. If the bag says French, Italian or espresso roast and the beans look shiny and oily, some bitterness is simply part of the coffee. Try a medium roast for a week. If the bitterness goes away, the brewing was never the problem. Blends with a lot of robusta are also naturally more bitter than 100% arabica.

6. The beans are stale

Old coffee loses its sweetness and fruity flavors first, so the bitterness that was always there stands out. Whole beans taste best within about two to four weeks of the roast date, and pre-ground coffee goes flat within days of opening. Look for a roast date (not just a best-by date) on the bag, buy amounts you'll finish in a few weeks, and keep beans in an airtight container away from heat and light.

7. The brewer or grinder is dirty

Coffee oil builds up on everything it touches: the carafe, the brew basket, the French press filter, the grinder burrs. Old oil goes rancid and tastes bitter and stale, and it gets into every new cup. A carafe with a brown film or a basket that smells like old coffee is a giveaway. Wash the carafe and basket after every use, descale a drip machine every one to three months depending on your water, and clean the grinder burrs every few weeks if you use it daily. For French press owners, we have a full French press cleaning guide (linked below).

8. It sat on the hot plate

The warming plate under a glass carafe keeps cooking the coffee. After 20 to 30 minutes it starts to taste burnt and bitter, and after an hour most people can taste it. Turn the plate off and pour the coffee into a thermal carafe or insulated mug, or brew a smaller pot more often.

9. The water itself

Coffee is about 98% water, so off-tasting water means off-tasting coffee. Heavily chlorinated tap water can taste harsh and chemical. A simple pitcher filter usually fixes it. Very hard water makes coffee taste flat and chalky and scales up machines faster. Don't use distilled water on its own, though: with no minerals at all, coffee tends to taste flat and sour.

Quick fixes for a cup that's already bitter

  • Add a tiny pinch of salt to the cup or the grounds. Salt dulls our perception of bitterness without making the coffee taste salty.
  • Add milk or cream. Fat and protein soften bitter flavors.
  • Dilute it with a little hot water. Over-strong coffee is often bitter coffee.
  • Pour it over ice. Cold coffee tastes less bitter than hot coffee.

Tip: Change one thing at a time. If you go coarser, use cooler water and switch beans all on the same morning, you won't know which change worked. Make one change, brew two or three times, then decide.

Why is my coffee bitter all of a sudden?

If nothing else changed, look at your gear and beans. A machine with mineral build-up, a grinder full of old oil, or a bag that's been open too long are the usual causes of a sudden change. New beans that are roasted darker can do it too.

Does adding salt to coffee reduce bitterness?

Yes. A small pinch, a few grains rather than a shake, reduces how bitter coffee tastes without making it salty. It's a quick fix for one cup, not a substitute for fixing the grind or brew time.

Is bitter coffee a sign of bad beans?

Not always. Most bitterness comes from brewing: a fine grind, a long brew or very hot water. Very dark roasts, blends with a lot of robusta, and stale beans are more bitter even when brewed well.

Why does my coffee taste bitter and sour at the same time?

That usually means uneven extraction, most often from a blade grinder that makes both fine dust and large chunks. The dust over-extracts and the chunks under-extract. A burr grinder is the real fix.

Why is my French press coffee bitter?

Usually because the coffee keeps brewing after you plunge. Pour it all into mugs or a carafe right away. A grind that's too fine, which also makes the plunger hard to push, is the other common cause.
