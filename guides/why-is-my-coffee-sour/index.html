Why Does My Coffee Taste Sour? 8 Causes and How to Fix Each

Why does my coffee taste sour? Usually under-extraction. Learn to tell sour from bright, then fix it by method: drip, pour-over, French press and espresso.

Sour coffee is almost always under-extracted coffee. The water didn't pull enough out of the grounds, so you taste the acids that dissolve first without the sugars and body that come later. The fix usually runs in one direction: grind a step or two finer, brew a little longer, or use hotter water. Start with the grind, because it's the biggest lever and the easiest to change.

Before you change anything, make sure the cup really is sour and not just bright, because a light roast is supposed to taste a little tangy. Below is a quick taste check, the eight causes in the order worth checking, and the first fix to try for each brew method.

Sour, bright or bitter? Check this first

Sour is a sharp, puckering taste you feel at the sides of the tongue, like an unripe lemon or a sip of vinegar. It usually comes with a thin, watery body and a short, salty or empty finish. Bright (what coffee bags call acidity) is a lively, juicy tang, more like an apple or a ripe orange, and it ends sweet. Bitter is the opposite problem: a dry, heavy harshness at the back of the tongue.

A simple test: brew the same coffee again with the grind one step finer and nothing else changed. If the sharpness drops and the cup tastes sweeter and fuller, it was under-extraction. If it turns harsh and dry, the first cup was fine and you were tasting the bean's natural acidity.

The 8 causes, in the order to check them

1. The grind is too coarse

Big particles have less surface area, so water can't extract them fully in the time it has. In drip and pour-over, coarse grounds also let the water rush through too fast, which cuts the contact time as well. Move one or two settings finer and brew again. If you use pre-ground coffee, check the label: coffee ground for a French press is too coarse for a drip machine or a cone.

A clue with pour-over: a single cup drains in well under 2 minutes.

2. The brew time is too short

Every method needs a minimum amount of time for the sweeter, heavier compounds to dissolve. Rough targets:

  • French press: 4 minutes before you plunge. Plunging at 2 or 3 minutes is a common reason it tastes sour.
  • Pour-over (one cup, V60 style): about 2:30 to 3:30 total.
  • Auto-drip machine: about 4 to 6 minutes for a full pot.
  • AeroPress: 1 to 2 minutes, a little longer with a coarser grind.
  • Espresso: about 25 to 30 seconds. A shot that gushes out in 15 seconds will be sour.
  • Cold brew: 12 to 18 hours in the fridge. Under about 10 hours it often tastes thin and tart.

3. The water isn't hot enough

The usual target is 195 to 205°F (90 to 96°C). Cooler water extracts more slowly, so a cup can taste sour even with a good grind and time. This catches a lot of people who pour from a kettle that has sat for a few minutes, or who use a cheap drip machine that never gets the water that hot. Light roasts are the most sensitive. Try 205°F for a light roast and around 200°F for a medium.

With a stovetop kettle, pour within about 30 seconds of the boil, and preheat a French press so the cold glass doesn't steal heat. If an older drip machine has started brewing cooler and slower, descale it.

4. Too little coffee for the water

A weak ratio tastes thin and watery, and with nothing to balance them the acids stand out as sour. A good starting point is 1:16 by weight, which is about 60 to 65 g of coffee per liter of water, or 2 level tablespoons per 6 oz cup. If you've been eyeballing scoops, weigh one: light-roast beans are denser, so a scoop weighs more than the same scoop of dark roast.

5. The roast is light (and the brew isn't adjusted for it)

Light-roasted beans are denser and harder to extract than dark ones. Brew a light roast the way you'd brew a dark roast and it will often come out sour. Use water close to 205°F, a slightly finer grind and a little more time. If you just don't like a tangy cup, try a medium roast from Brazil, Sumatra or Colombia, which tend to be lower in acidity than light-roasted Kenyan or Ethiopian coffee.

6. Uneven extraction

If part of the coffee bed is under-extracted, the cup tastes sour even when the average is right. The usual causes are a blade grinder that leaves big chunks next to fine dust, dry clumps of grounds in a French press that never got wet, and a pour-over where the water tunnels down one side. Fixes: use a burr grinder, stir the French press gently after you add the water, and pour in slow circles over the whole bed, not straight down the middle.

In espresso this shows up as channeling: water finds a weak path through the puck and rushes through it. Distribute the grounds evenly in the basket before you tamp, and tamp level.

7. The coffee sat too long or was reheated

Brewed coffee slowly turns more sour and stale as it sits, and reheating makes it worse. A thermal carafe holds it much better than a hot plate. If it tasted fine at 7 a.m. and sour at 9, the brewing was never the problem.

8. The water has too few minerals

Minerals in water help pull flavor out of coffee. Distilled and plain reverse-osmosis water extract poorly and tend to taste flat and sour, and very soft tap water can too. Filtered tap water or spring water is usually the better choice.

Espresso tastes sour? Read this

Sour espresso is the most common complaint from new home baristas, and it nearly always means the shot ran too fast. A classic starting recipe is 18 g of coffee in, about 36 g of liquid out, in 25 to 30 seconds. If you're getting 36 g in 15 seconds, grind finer. Change the grind in small steps and pull a couple of shots at each setting before you judge.

If you can't grind fine enough to slow the shot down, your grinder probably isn't made for espresso. Beans roasted only 3 or 4 days ago can also pull sour, so give them a week.

Quick fixes for a cup that's already sour

  • Add milk or cream. Fat and protein soften the sharp edge.
  • Add a very small pinch of salt. It tames sourness and bitterness without making the cup salty.
  • Add a little sugar or honey. Sweetness balances acidity, which is why sour coffee tastes thinner than it should.
  • Don't add more hot water. Diluting a sour cup makes it thinner and the sourness more obvious.

Tip: Change one thing at a time. If you go finer, hotter and longer on the same morning, you may overshoot straight into bitter and won't know which change mattered. Make one change, brew two or three times, then decide.

Is sour coffee under-extracted or over-extracted?

Under-extracted, in almost every case. The acids in coffee dissolve first, and the sugars and heavier compounds that balance them come later. Stop too early and the acids dominate. Bitterness is the sign of over-extraction.

If my coffee is sour, should I grind finer or coarser?

Finer. A finer grind extracts faster and, in drip and pour-over, slows the water down so it spends more time with the coffee. Go one or two steps at a time. If the cup turns harsh and dry, you went too far, so come back one step.

Why does my coffee suddenly taste sour?

If you didn't change anything on purpose, look at what changed by accident: a new bag of lighter-roasted beans, a grinder setting that got bumped, a drip machine with scale build-up that now brews cooler, or a switch to bottled or distilled water.

Can old coffee beans taste sour?

Stale beans mostly taste flat and papery, though some people describe them as sour. Brewed coffee that has sat for an hour or been reheated does turn sour. Very fresh beans, roasted only a few days ago, can also taste sour, especially as espresso.

Is sour coffee bad for you?

No. Under-extracted coffee is a taste problem, not a safety one. The exception is coffee left out for many hours or brewed in a dirty, moldy machine. Clean it and brew fresh.
