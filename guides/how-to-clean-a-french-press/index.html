How to Clean a French Press: Daily Rinse, Deep Clean and Fixes

How to clean a French press the right way: a 1-minute daily rinse, a weekly deep clean for glass and steel, drain-safe grounds disposal and stuck plunger fixes.

Here's the short answer. After every brew, get the grounds out (into the trash or compost, not the sink), rinse the carafe, and run hot water through the plunger. Once a week, unscrew the filter stack, soak the parts for 15 minutes in hot water with a little dish soap, scrub the mesh with a soft brush, and let everything dry fully before you put it back together.

That routine takes about a minute a day and ten minutes a week, and it prevents the three problems that make people give up on a French press: stale, bitter flavors from old coffee oil, a plunger that sticks, and a musty smell from parts that never dry. The rest of this guide covers each step in detail, how care differs for glass and stainless steel presses, and what to do when something has already gone wrong.

What you need

  • Dish soap and hot water. This handles 90% of the job.
  • A soft bottle brush or long-handled sponge that reaches the bottom of the carafe.
  • An old soft toothbrush for the mesh screens.
  • A silicone spatula or spoon to scrape out grounds.
  • Baking soda, for oily film and stains.
  • White vinegar, only if you have hard-water spots (more on that below).
  • Optional: a coffee-equipment cleaning powder such as Urnex Cafiza, which dissolves coffee oils better than dish soap.

Skip steel wool, scouring powder and the green side of a kitchen sponge. They scratch glass, and scratches are where coffee oil and stains settle in.

Step 1: Get the grounds out without clogging your drain

Coffee grounds don't dissolve. In a drain they clump together with grease and soap scum and slowly build a plug, and plumbers list them among the usual suspects in clogged kitchen drains. They're also hard on septic systems. Keep them out of the sink, even with a garbage disposal.

  • Let the press cool for a few minutes after you pour, then pull the plunger out.
  • Scrape the wet puck into the trash or compost bin with a silicone spatula. Most of it comes out in one or two scoops.
  • For the last bit, add a splash of water, swirl, and pour it through a fine mesh strainer held over the bin or sink. Tip the strainer into the bin.

Step 2: The daily rinse (about 1 minute)

  • Rinse the carafe with hot water and wipe the inside with a soft brush or sponge and a drop of dish soap.
  • Hold the plunger under hot running water and pump it up and down a few times in the full carafe, or swish the filter around in soapy water. This flushes most of the grounds trapped between the screens.
  • Rinse everything with clean hot water so no soap taste is left behind.
  • Leave the plunger out of the carafe, or stand it with the filter above the rim, so air can reach both. Don't seal a wet press with the lid on.

Cleaning while the press is still warm is easier, because coffee oil is softer and wipes off without scrubbing. If you're in a rush, at least get the grounds out and fill the press with hot water until you can come back to it.

Step 3: The weekly deep clean (about 10 minutes)

The daily rinse doesn't reach the oil that builds up between the filter layers. Over a week or two that oil goes rancid and gives your coffee a flat, stale taste, even with fresh beans. It's also what makes a plunger feel gritty. Once a week, or every 10 to 15 brews, take the filter apart.

  • Unscrew the filter stack from the bottom of the plunger rod. On most presses, including the Veken, Bodum and Utopia models below, it comes apart into a spiral spring plate, one or more mesh screens and a cross plate.
  • Lay the parts out in order, or snap a photo with your phone, so you can rebuild the stack the same way.
  • Soak the parts and the carafe for 15 minutes in hot water with dish soap. For heavy buildup, use a coffee cleaning powder instead, following its label (usually about 1 teaspoon per liter of hot water).
  • Scrub both sides of each mesh screen with a soft toothbrush, working along the weave. Brush the threads on the rod and the underside of the lid, where splashes collect.
  • Rinse every part well and hold the mesh up to the light. If you can still see brown in the weave, soak and brush again.
  • Dry everything completely on a towel or rack before you screw the stack back together. Trapped water between the layers is the main cause of a musty smell and mold on old filters.

Glass carafes: keep them clear and crack-free

Borosilicate glass handles heat well, but it doesn't handle sudden temperature changes or knocks against a faucet. Never pour cold water into a hot glass carafe, and never pour boiling water into one that's just come out of a cold room or the fridge. Let it come to room temperature first. The sink, not the brewing, is where glass carafes are most at risk.

If the glass has gone cloudy or has a brown film, make a paste of 1 tablespoon of baking soda and a little water, rub it around with a soft sponge, and rinse. Baking soda is mildly abrasive but soft enough not to scratch. If the cloudiness is white and chalky instead, that's mineral scale from hard water, and vinegar is the better fix (see below).

Stainless steel presses: the inside you can't see

A double-walled steel press can't shatter, which removes the biggest risk of cleaning. The catch is that you can't see buildup, so oil and stains collect quietly. Look inside with a flashlight once a week. If the walls look brown or feel slick, use the baking soda paste or a cleaning powder soak and a long bottle brush.

  • Don't use bleach or chlorine cleaners on stainless steel. They can pit the surface.
  • Don't leave coffee sitting in a steel press overnight. It keeps the walls stained and makes the next cup taste old.
  • Dry the rim and the seam at the spout. That's where water spots and rust-colored marks start on cheaper steel.
  • Check your manual before using a dishwasher. The double wall can trap water if the seal at the base isn't perfect.

When to use vinegar (and when not to)

Vinegar is good at one thing here: dissolving the white mineral scale that hard water leaves on glass and steel. It does little for coffee oil, which is the more common problem, so don't reach for it by default. If you do have scale, fill the carafe with equal parts white vinegar and hot water, let it sit for 15 to 20 minutes, scrub lightly, and rinse two or three times. Any vinegar smell is gone once it's rinsed and dried. Avoid long vinegar soaks on the metal frame and plunger parts, and never mix vinegar with baking soda in a closed press, since the fizz builds pressure.

How to fix a stuck or stiff plunger

A plunger that fights you is a warning sign. Forcing it can bend the rod, push coffee out of the spout, or crack a glass carafe. Work through these causes in order.

  • The grind is too fine. Fine grounds pack against the mesh like mud. Grind one or two steps coarser, close to coarse sea salt.
  • The mesh is clogged with old oil. Take the filter apart and do the deep clean above. This fixes most stiff plungers.
  • The filter layers are out of order or not seated flat. Rebuild the stack in its original order (this is where that photo helps), and check that the edges of the mesh lie flat against the carafe wall.
  • The mesh is bent or torn. If the edge of the screen is crumpled, grounds slip past it and the plunger tilts. Replacement screens are inexpensive for most brands.
  • The rod is bent. Roll it on a flat counter. If it wobbles, it needs replacing; it will only get worse.

If you're mid-brew and the plunger stops, pull it up an inch, then press again slowly. Don't lean on it with your body weight.

Common mistakes

  • Dumping grounds in the sink. It works for a while, then it doesn't. Use the trash or compost.
  • Only rinsing, never disassembling. The filter looks clean from the outside long after the inside has gone stale.
  • Reassembling while wet and putting the lid on. That's how mold starts. Store the press open and the filter dry.
  • Ignoring a damaged screen. A torn or bent mesh lets sediment through and won't get better with cleaning.

Tip: Run the deep clean on the same day each week, for example Sunday morning, and keep a spare mesh screen in the drawer. Swapping in the spare while the other soaks means you never skip coffee to clean.

How often should I clean a French press?

Rinse it after every use and take the filter apart for a deep clean once a week, or every 10 to 15 brews. If you brew daily with dark, oily roasts, go to twice a week.

Is a French press dishwasher safe?

Many glass carafes and stainless filter parts are, but it depends on the brand, so check the manual. Hand washing is gentler: dishwashers can cloud glass, wear down plastic lids and leave detergent in the mesh. Never put a wooden or cork handle in the dishwasher.

Can I clean a French press with vinegar or baking soda?

Yes. Use baking soda paste for brown oily film and stains, and a 50/50 white vinegar and hot water soak for white hard-water scale. Rinse well afterward and neither will affect the taste of your coffee.

What should I do with French press coffee grounds?

Scrape them into the trash or compost and strain any leftover slurry through a mesh strainer. Don't wash them down the sink or garbage disposal, where they clump and clog pipes over time.

When should I replace the French press filter?

Replace the mesh screen when it's torn, its edge is bent so it doesn't sit flat, or you're getting noticeably more grit than before. With weekly cleaning, a steel screen usually lasts years.
