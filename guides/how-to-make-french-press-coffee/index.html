How to Make French Press Coffee: Ratio, Grind and Timing

How to make French press coffee step by step: a gram chart for 1 to 4 cups, the right grind and timing, and quick fixes for bitter or muddy coffee.

Here's the short answer. Use 1 gram of coarsely ground coffee for every 15 grams of water (about 1:15), pour water at roughly 200°F, stir, put the lid on and wait 4 minutes. Then press slowly and pour all of the coffee out right away. For one big mug, that's 20 g of coffee and 300 g of water.

That method works in any French press. The rest of this guide shows how to scale it for 1 to 4 cups, how to dial it in when it tastes off, and a slower method that gives a noticeably cleaner cup if you have 10 minutes.

How much coffee to use: a chart for 1 to 4 cups

Weighing is the easiest way to get the same cup every day, because a tablespoon of coarse grounds can weigh anywhere from about 4 to 7 grams depending on the bean and grind. If you don't have a scale yet, the tablespoon column below assumes roughly 5 g per level tablespoon of ground coffee. Water weight and volume are the same thing here: 1 gram of water is 1 milliliter.

Want it stronger? Move toward 1:12 (25 g per 300 g of water). Want it lighter? Go to 1:17 (about 18 g per 300 g). Change the ratio before you change anything else, because it's the easiest variable to repeat. Leave about an inch of space at the top of the press so the lid and plunger fit without pushing coffee over the rim.

What you need

  • A French press. A 34 oz press makes 2 to 4 mugs, and a 12 oz press makes one.
  • Whole-bean coffee, ideally roasted within the last month.
  • A burr grinder that can grind coarse and evenly. This matters more than the press itself.
  • A kettle. Any kettle works here, because a French press doesn't need a slow gooseneck pour.
  • A kitchen or coffee scale (optional but helpful) and a spoon for stirring.

Step by step: the classic 4-minute method

  • Heat the water. Bring it to a boil, then let it sit for about 30 seconds so it drops to around 200°F (93°C). Anywhere from 195°F to 205°F is fine.
  • Preheat the press. Swirl a little hot water in the empty press and pour it out. Cold glass steals heat fast, especially in a big press making a small batch.
  • Grind the coffee coarse, about the texture of coarse sea salt or breadcrumbs. Grind right before brewing so it doesn't go stale.
  • Add the grounds and start a timer. Pour in all the water at a steady pace, aiming to wet every bit of coffee.
  • Stir gently at about 30 seconds. A few slow stirs sink the floating crust so all the grounds steep evenly.
  • Put the lid on with the plunger pulled all the way up, and wait until the timer reads 4:00.
  • Press slowly. Push the plunger down with gentle, steady pressure over about 20 to 30 seconds. If it fights you hard, the grind is too fine.
  • Pour it all out right away. Coffee left sitting on the grounds keeps brewing and turns bitter, so decant extra into a thermos or carafe.

For a cleaner cup: the slow skim method

Coffee YouTuber James Hoffmann popularized a slower French press technique that cuts down on sediment without changing your gear. It takes 9 to 12 minutes, but most of that is waiting. Try it on a weekend and see if you prefer it.

  • Brew exactly as above up to the 4-minute mark, but don't put the lid on yet.
  • At 4:00, stir the crust on top. Most of the grounds will sink to the bottom.
  • Scoop off the foam and the few floating bits with two spoons.
  • Wait another 5 to 8 minutes. The fine particles settle to the bottom during this time.
  • Put the lid on and lower the plunger only until the screen sits just below the surface of the coffee. Don't press down into the grounds; the screen is only there as a strainer.
  • Pour slowly and leave the last splash in the press, since that's where the silt collects.

The coffee will be a little cooler by the time you drink it, around a good sipping temperature for most people. If you like it very hot, preheat your mug too.

Common mistakes and how to fix them

  • Bitter or harsh: the grind is too fine, the water is too hot, or the coffee sat on the grounds too long. Grind one step coarser, let boiling water rest 30 seconds, and pour everything out after pressing.
  • Sour, thin or weak: the opposite problem. Grind a step finer, use a bit more coffee (try 1:13), or steep for 5 minutes instead of 4.
  • Muddy or gritty: fines are slipping through the mesh. A burr grinder helps most. Also press gently, skip the last half inch of the pour, or try the slow skim method. A thin layer of silt is normal with any metal filter.
  • The plunger is hard to push: the grind is too fine, or old coffee oils have gummed up the screen. Coarsen the grind and take the filter apart to scrub it every week or two.
  • Coffee spurts out the sides of the screen: the filter isn't sitting flat, or the mesh is bent. Reassemble the filter stack and check that the edges are straight. Replacement screens are cheap for most presses.
  • Stale flavor: pre-ground coffee loses its aroma within days of opening. Buy whole beans in amounts you'll finish within 2 to 3 weeks.

Tip: Clean the press while it's still warm. Dump the grounds into the trash or compost (not down the sink, where they can clog drains), then unscrew the filter stack and rinse each screen. Leftover oil on the mesh is what makes old presses taste stale.

Glass or stainless steel?

Glass lets you watch the brew and doesn't hold onto flavors, but it can crack if it's knocked against a sink or hit with cold water while hot. Double-walled stainless steel can't shatter and holds heat much longer, which helps with the slow method and with big 4-cup batches. If your press lives near a busy sink, or goes camping, steel is the safer choice.

How much coffee do I put in a French press?

Use about 1 g of coffee per 15 g of water. That's 20 g (about 4 level tablespoons) for one 10 oz mug and 60 g (about 12 tablespoons) for a full 34 oz press.

How long should French press coffee steep?

Four minutes is the standard starting point. Go to 5 minutes if the coffee tastes weak or sour, or drop to 3 minutes and a slightly coarser grind if it tastes bitter.

What grind size is best for a French press?

Coarse, about the texture of coarse sea salt. On the OXO Brew that's near the coarse end of the dial, and on most burr grinders it's the last quarter of the range. Fine grinds make bitter, muddy coffee and a plunger that's hard to push.

Can I use pre-ground coffee in a French press?

Yes, but buy a bag labeled for French press or coarse grind. Regular pre-ground coffee is ground for drip machines, which is too fine and will make a muddy cup.

Should I stir French press coffee?

Yes, once or twice gently after adding the water. It sinks the dry crust so all the coffee steeps evenly. Avoid stirring hard right before you press, which kicks up fine particles.
